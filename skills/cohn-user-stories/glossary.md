# Glossary — User Stories Applied (Mike Cohn)

**Acceptance test** — Customer-specified criterion proving a story meets her expectations; lives on the back of the card (the "Confirmation"). (Ch 1, Ch 6, Ch 21)
**Card / Conversation / Confirmation (3 Cs)** — Ron Jeffries' three aspects of a story: the written reminder, the detail-fleshing dialogue, and the acceptance tests. (Ch 1)
**Closed story** — A story that ends at a meaningful, completable goal giving a sense of accomplishment (Lauesen). (Ch 7)
**Combining stories** — Merging several too-small stories into one schedulable story (~half-day to several days). (Ch 2)
**Completism** — The compulsion to fill every field of a template; a use-case hazard stories escape (Fowler). (Ch 13)
**Complex story** — An epic that is inherently large and resists clean splitting; split into spike + development story. (Ch 2)
**Compound story** — An epic bundling multiple independent shorter stories; split along create/edit/delete or data boundaries. (Ch 2)
**Constraint** — A nonfunctional requirement written on a card annotated "Constraint"; not estimated/scheduled, but enforced with automated tests. (Ch 7, Ch 16)
**Context-free question** — A question containing no implied answer or preference. (Ch 4)
**Customer** — The role that writes and prioritizes stories and writes/executes acceptance tests; may be a user, product manager, analyst, or a "one-voice" customer team. (Ch 1, Ch 5, App A)
**Customer team** — Those who ensure the software meets users' needs (testers, product manager, real users, interaction designers). (Ch 1, Ch 5)
**Daily burndown chart** — Plot of hours *remaining* in the iteration, updated daily on a shared whiteboard. (Ch 11)
**Developer team** — Those who build and estimate the software. (Ch 1)
**Disaggregation** — Breaking a user-valued story into developer tasks; "just-in-time design." (Ch 10)
**Empirical design** — Designing by studying/interviewing users who never become true participants (contrast: participatory). (Ch 13)
**Epic** — A story too large to be useful; split before working on it. (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 13)
**Essential use case** — A use case stripped of UI/technology, written as User Intention / System Responsibility columns (Constantine & Lockwood). (Ch 12)
**Extensions** — A use case's alternate/error paths; map onto a story's acceptance tests. (Ch 12)
**Extreme characters** — Designing for exaggerated personalities to surface overlooked stories (Djajadiningrat et al.). (Ch 3)
**FIT / FitNesse** — Framework for Integrated Test (Ward Cunningham); customer writes tabular tests, programmer writes fixtures; green/red results. (Ch 6)
**Goal story** — A high-level story derived from a user role's goal; seeds lower-level stories. (Ch 7)
**Goldplating** — Adding unrequested features or over-implementing a story. (Ch 14)
**IEEE 830** — 1998 software-requirements-spec standard; signature form "The system shall…". (Ch 12, Ch 13)
**Ideal time vs. elapsed time** — Uninterrupted work time vs. wall-clock time including interruptions; estimate in ideal, convert via velocity. (Ch 8)
**INVEST** — Bill Wake's checklist for a good story: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable. (Ch 2, Ch 7)
**Iteration** — A fixed timebox (1–4 weeks) delivering working, tested, usable code. (Ch 1, Ch 10, App A)
**Iteration burndown chart** — Plot of story points *remaining* in the release at each iteration's end; absorbs scope change but hides speed. (Ch 11)
**Iterative vs. incremental** — Refining the whole through passes vs. finishing one complete piece at a time; Scrum/XP do both. (Ch 15)
**Low-fidelity prototype** — A paper/whiteboard map of conceptual workflows (boxes + arrows), *not* screens; thrown away within days. (Ch 4)
**Main success scenario** — The primary successful path through a use case. (Ch 12)
**MoSCoW** — DSDM prioritization: Must / Should / Could / Won't have this time. (Ch 9, Ch 20)
**Net progress** — Completed points − changed-estimate increases − new-story points. (Ch 11)
**Nonfunctional requirement (NFR)** — A requirement about the software but not its functionality; made testable or captured as a constraint. (Ch 2, Ch 16)
**NUTs** — "Nebulous Units of Time," a wry label for story points (Kerievsky). (Ch 8)
**Open-ended question** — A question inviting an in-depth response, no implied answer. (Ch 4)
**Opportunistic development** — Developers moving freely among requirements, design, and scenarios rather than top-down (Guindon; Parnas & Clements). (Ch 13)
**Parking lot** — A visible list of mid-workshop tangents to revisit later. (Ch 4)
**Participatory design** — Engaging users *as members* of the design team. (Ch 13)
**Persona** — An imaginary, named, photographed, backstoried representation of an important user role (Cooper). (Ch 3, Ch 17)
**Planning Game** — XP's collaborative release/iteration planning ritual. (App A)
**Planning poker** — Iterative team estimation: private estimate → simultaneous reveal → discuss high/low → re-estimate until convergence (a.k.a. Wideband Delphi). (Ch 8, Ch 19)
**Product backlog** — Scrum's master prioritized list of all desired functionality; reprioritizable any time by the product owner. (Ch 15)
**Product owner** — Scrum's equivalent of XP's customer; owns and prioritizes the product backlog. (Ch 15)
**Project champion** — A single customer *voice* ("first among equals") for the customer team. (Ch 5)
**Refactoring** — Restructuring code without changing external behavior; required, not optional, in XP. (App A)
**Release** — One or more iterations whose stories define a shippable plan (a revisable hypothesis). (Ch 1, Ch 9, App A)
**Role attribute** — A distinguishing fact about a role (frequency of use, domain expertise, computer proficiency, goal). (Ch 3)
**Role card** — A note card holding a role name plus its attributes. (Ch 3)
**ScrumMaster** — Servant-leader/facilitator who removes obstacles and enforces Scrum's rules. (Ch 15)
**Simple design** — Beck's 4 constraints: conveys intent, no duplication, fewest classes, fewest methods. (App A)
**Slice the cake** — Splitting a large story into thin *vertical* slices through all layers, not horizontal technical layers (Bill Wake). (Ch 7, Ch 14)
**Smell** — An observable symptom that something is amiss in how stories are applied; not itself a defect. (Ch 14)
**Spike** — A brief, timeboxed experiment to learn enough to estimate or de-risk a story. (Ch 2, App A)
**Sprint** — Scrum's (classically 30-day) iteration. (Ch 15)
**Sprint backlog** — Tasks committed for the current sprint; only the team may add mid-sprint. (Ch 15)
**Sprint goal** — A short statement of a sprint's aim; success is judged against it. (Ch 15)
**Story points** — A relative measure of a story's size/complexity/effort, defined per team. (Ch 1, Ch 8)
**Sustainable pace** — A brisk but consistent work pace (≈6 hrs/day pairing) that avoids burnout. (App A)
**Task** — A unit of work derived from a story; estimated in ideal hours, owned by an individual. (Ch 10)
**Theme** — A main focus area for an upcoming release; part of a product roadmap (Beck). (Ch 9)
**Timebox** — The maximum time allotted (to a spike or an iteration), making the unestimatable estimatable. (Ch 2)
**Trawling** — Active, repeated gathering of requirements that change over time (Robertson & Robertson), replacing "elicitation." (Ch 4)
**Triangulation** — Estimating a story by comparison to already-estimated stories. (Ch 8)
**Use case** — A generalized description of system–actor interactions (Jacobson; Cockburn); persistent; larger than a story. (Ch 12)
**User proxy** — Someone representing users who may not be a user themselves; each type has a predictable bias. (Ch 5)
**User role** — A collection of attributes characterizing a population of users and their intended interactions. (Ch 3)
**User role modeling** — Constantine & Lockwood's brainstorm → organize → consolidate → refine process for identifying roles. (Ch 3, Ch 17)
**User task force** — Real users used as a sounding board while a proxy retains final decisions. (Ch 5)
**Velocity** — Story points of *completed* stories per iteration; converts points into a calendar. (Ch 1, Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 11)
