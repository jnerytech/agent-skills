# Chapter 12: Case studies & concluding thoughts

## Core Idea
Six teams in radically different domains — price comparison, data archiving, student lending, airline operations, pension services, and a music startup — each arrived at the same patterns (deriving scope from goals, collaborating on specifications, executable specifications, living documentation) by trial and error, without copying any "best practice." This proves the practices are contextual but durable: there is no single recipe, yet the same forces produce the same results everywhere.

## Case Studies

### uSwitch
- **Context**: One of the busiest UK websites — price comparison for energy, credit cards, insurance. Complex external-partner integrations + high scalability + a 10-year legacy with no reliable docs. Three-year overhaul of the whole delivery process.
- **What changed**: Started with separate QA team running unmaintainable QTP tests (bottleneck, 3-day deploys). Merged testers into developers (dropped the tester title — quality is everyone's problem). Selenium → Cucumber to get human-readable tests, which sparked test-first executable specs and specification workshops. Built a dedicated continuous-validation environment to kill flakiness; raised abstraction in tests ("user enters a valid amount" not "enters 100 in box _id"); business users took ownership of stories ('so that' from business, 'I want' from devs). Moved from iterations to deploy-on-demand flow, backed by production user-experience monitoring.
- **Key lessons**: (1) Focus on improving *quality*, not implementing a named process — constantly find the single most important obstacle and fix it, then re-inspect. (2) Change is initially tool-driven but mostly *cultural* — removing the tester/developer divide enabled business-value focus and business engagement. (3) Their controversial move: executable specs are truly *executable, not obligatory* — they disable low-risk tests after a feature ships (slow feedback from an ever-growing suite costs more than the regression protection in low-risk areas), relying on production monitoring instead.

### RainStor
- **Context**: UK high-capacity data-archiving product. Technical domain — complexity from huge data volumes, performance targets, compression algorithms. Fewer than 30 staff, one Scrum team. Adoption was organic and tester-driven; "Nobody else in the company knows what ATDD means."
- **What changed**: Escaped a mini-waterfall (frozen requirements doc doubling as tech spec). Built a simple homegrown automated test harness to push testing earlier and align dev/test. Switched to user stories, broke them into small deliverables, illustrated even performance with examples ("import N records in 10 minutes on M CPUs"). Reached out to actual customers for realistic data sets and expected targets, feeding them straight into the harness as acceptance tests. Split continuous validation into regular (<1hr) / overnight / weekend builds by data-set size; ran tests manually until stable, then promoted them.
- **Key lessons**: (1) Executable specs eliminated maintaining two document sets and killed wasteful rework. (2) User stories engaged business users better. (3) Deriving scope from business goals with high-level examples ensured they built the right product. (4) Engaging customers in specifications got acceptance criteria from the start, guaranteeing targets were met. Overarching: focus on principles, treat community practices only as *inspiration*, and build your own tool to fit the process if needed.

### Iowa Student Loan
- **Context**: Financial-services company; pushes SBE "to the limit." Complex data-driven domain from public loan-request site to back-office underwriting/origination. Rolled practices from small projects into a 3-year rewrite of the entire underwriting platform.
- **What changed**: Started with XP and very technical HTTPUnit tests no one could read. Brought in consultants (Rainsberger → FitNesse; Hussman → write tests businesspeople can read unaided). Adopted user *personas* to find the right abstraction and the correct application entry point — which cured "fantasy state" (false-green tests that passed while the browser failed). Re-routed automation through real production flows via lightweight HTTP fixtures, exposing hidden bugs and slashing maintenance. Consolidated/split specs to keep build time constant; wrote a FitNesse version-control plug-in and an automated check for disabled tests; built a "business framework" (stable FitNesse entry pages mapping system flow to tests) = the start of a real living documentation system.
- **Living documentation as competitive advantage**: When the 2008 credit crisis killed their bond-sale funding model three months before go-live, they repurposed existing acceptance tests to analyze, implement, and verify a dramatic core overhaul (introducing the previously nonexistent "lender" concept) **in one month**. "Any other system that didn't have the tests would halt development and it would have been a rewrite."
- **Key lessons**: A tool alone won't bring business users in — specify from the *user's* perspective. Adoption here was driven not by quality or test automation but by the need for *relevant documentation*; the heavy investment in living documentation paid off by absorbing a business-model change. Open-source tools matter because you can modify them when they block collaboration.

### Sabre Airline Solutions
- **Context**: Software/services for airlines (plan, operate, sell). Early adopter of XP and SBE on a *massive* project — AirCentre Movement Manager — with a large distributed team. Two prior attempts to build the system failed on domain complexity and quality.
- **What changed**: Abandoned a unit-test tool that didn't drive collaboration; tried FIT in 2003 but focused on the tool, not practices (clients never wrote FIT tests; devs wrote them alone). Brought in ObjectMentor to train *everyone*, creating a common baseline and a believer core group. Proved the approach on a small, low-risk internal project first, learning that the automation layer is real code (keep fixtures simple, kill duplication) before scaling to a 30-person, two-continent, two-year C++→Java rewrite.
- **Improving collaboration**: Initially split into core / UI / integration teams — but customers only engaged once they saw a UI, so back-end teams missed expectations and got story explosions and rework. ~6 months in they reorganized into **end-to-end feature teams**, letting business users engage with every team. Halved iterations to one week; when testers became the acceptance-test bottleneck, they had developers help write tests. Pairing a developer and a tester produced far better tests than either alone — "the trust was amazing." Williams's data-driven tips: hide incidental data, remove duplication, look for duplication during incremental development, refactor tests like code, and isolate/mock uncontrollable third-party host systems.
- **Key lessons**: Focusing on a tool inside a closed group fails — get *everyone* engaged. Use a small low-risk project as a learning catalyst for the big one. Feature teams beat component teams for business engagement. Tester+developer collaboration on tests produces better specs and builds trust. SBE conquered a complex domain by giving development a clear, continuously validated target.

### ePlan Services
- **Context**: Denver 401(k) retirement pension provider serving small employers; cheaper operations = competitive advantage, so business-process automation is core. Agile tester Lisa Crispin. Uses living documentation to handle a complex domain and transfer knowledge.
- **What changed**: Mike Cohn introduced Scrum; they were burning a fifth of each iteration on manual testing. Got unit testing/TDD right first (most bugs were unit-level), then functional smoke tests, then FitNesse — Crispin grabbed a different story+developer to pair on each sprint, finding requirement-misunderstanding bugs immediately so devs saw the value. Over-specified compliance algorithm tests up front ("couldn't see the forest for the trees"), then shifted to one high-level test up front + a happy-path test paired at pickup + tester-extended examples = just-in-time specs. Documented specialist knowledge of a departing senior dev, then other legacy areas — which exposed real inconsistencies (e.g., five off-system bank accounts reconciled by email/phone).
- **Key lessons**: They moved away from manual testing for cheaper, faster delivery, but discovered the *shared understanding* from collaboration ("the real value is that we're talking") matters more than the automation. Specify key examples, not every combination, to give developers a usable target. A consistent living documentation system captures specialist knowledge and exposes inconsistencies/gaps in the actual business processes. Crispin's ah-ha: "I didn't own quality" — her job was to help the whole team define and ensure it; developers must be engaged; the process needs patience and baby steps.

### Songkick
- **Context**: UK live-music consumer startup — the *only* case with no large legacy system, and the one where user experience/interaction is the most critical product aspect. Implemented SBE to deliver software that matters and to grow the team. "As a startup you can't afford to not be delivering value all the time" (CTO Phil Cowans).
- **What changed**: Grew "beyond the size where everyone can sit around a table," adopted TDD for confidence, then naturally extended TDD to business functionality via Kanban, user stories, and Cucumber — deriving scope from goals. Early mistakes: over-tested trivial UI (easy to think about) while neglecting edge cases; tied tests too closely to the UI so they were brittle (a punctuation change broke tests, pushing people to code-first/test-later). Fixed by making steps semantic and pushing domain↔UI translation into the automation layer. ~6 months to feel comfortable. A feeds rewrite, protected by business-focused specs, saved an estimated 50% of refactoring time; so few production issues they need no bug tracker.
- **No living documentation yet**: They added tests ad hoc per feature and never designed a high-level structure, so it's hard to find which tests cover which functionality — only now starting to reorganize into a living documentation system organized around the site's feature set.
- **Key lessons**: Without a massive legacy system you can go from TDD to SBE quickly, as an extension of TDD. Treat the test suite as a first-class citizen needing the same care as production code. Tests are the canonical description of what the app does — building the right thing matters as much as building it well. Keep everyone involved; it's not just a developer activity.

## Cross-Case Patterns
- **Start small and improve continuously, never big-bang**: Every team looked for the single most important obstacle, fixed it, then re-inspected — and several proved practices on a small/low-risk project before scaling to a risky one (Sabre, Iowa).
- **Efficient functional test automation comes first and frees attention**: Until unit-/functional-level bugs stop consuming all the time, teams can't see the higher-order problems (ePlan, Songkick); aligning testing with development removes the end-of-iteration bottleneck (RainStor, uSwitch).
- **Tools start the change but the win is cultural**: Cucumber/FitNesse/FIT sparked collaboration, but real progress came from dropping role divisions, building trust, and getting business users to take ownership of specifications (uSwitch, Sabre, ePlan).
- **Automation must run through real business/production flows at the right abstraction**: Faking state or coupling to the UI/DOM produces false-green "fantasy state" and brittle, high-maintenance tests; routing through real entry points (personas, semantic steps, domain language) is what makes specs reliable and cheap to maintain (Iowa, Songkick, uSwitch).
- **Living documentation pays off long-term**: Reliable, business-aligned specs let teams absorb major change cheaply — a business-model pivot in a month (Iowa), surviving a key person's absence (ePlan), 50% faster refactoring (Songkick).

## Concluding Thoughts (Ch 18)
- **Collaboration on requirements builds trust** between stakeholders and the delivery team — and trust is the real prize: most orgs run on *mistrust* (sign-offs, change management, code freezes), which Adzic calls "alibi generators" — pure waste whose only purpose is ensuring nobody can be blamed. Collaborating on specifications dismantles that bureaucracy.
- **Collaboration requires preparation** — examples are a two-step process: one or two people prepare basic examples (answering obvious questions, suggesting a format) before the whole team discusses and extends them, making the workshop far more effective; vague requirements warrant ~two weeks of prep, stable ones a few days.
- **There are many different ways to collaborate** — big all-team workshops are great early for transferring domain knowledge and aligning everyone, but most teams drop them once trust and domain knowledge build, shifting to small "whoever has an interest" workshops or ad hoc chats, with the living documentation filling in everyone else.
- **Looking at the end goal as business process documentation is a useful model** — if specs document business processes, their model stays aligned with the business model so changes are *symmetric* (small business change = small spec change, no shotgun effect), they survive technology changes, and automation becomes merely the tool that exercises those processes on the software, not the goal.
- **Long-term value comes from living documentation** — short-term benefits (faster delivery, better quality) are universal, but teams who "cleaned up their tests" reaped outsized long-term value years later; new teams can get there faster by *intentionally* building a living documentation system rather than arriving by trial and error.

## Key Takeaways
1. There is no "best practice" — software development is deeply contextual; copy principles and forces, not another team's recipe.
2. Improve by relentlessly attacking the single biggest current obstacle, then re-inspecting — not by adopting a named process wholesale.
3. Get efficient test automation working first; it's the precondition that frees a team to see and solve the real problems.
4. The decisive shift is cultural — drop role silos, engage business users in owning specifications, and let trust replace bureaucratic "alibi generators."
5. Reliable specs require automation through real business processes at the right abstraction; brittle, UI-coupled, or state-faking tests destroy trust via false greens.
6. Living documentation is the long-game payoff — it absorbs business-model changes, preserves specialist knowledge, and slashes the cost of large rewrites.
7. Collaboration needs a preparation step and can take many forms; match the form (big workshop → small workshop → ad hoc chat) to the team's current trust and domain maturity.
