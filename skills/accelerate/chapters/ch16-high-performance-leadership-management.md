# Chapter 16: High-Performance Leadership and Management
*Authors: Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell (Part III; case study: ING Netherlands)*

## Core Idea
Technical excellence is necessary but not sufficient. Lasting advantage also needs a lightweight, high-performance management framework that connects strategy to action, speeds up feedback and learning, and is built through experimentation, not copied. "When you change the way you work, you change the routines, you create a different culture."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Leadership Obeya**: a glass-walled room that visualizes the tribe lead's work in **four zones**: *strategic improvement, performance monitoring, portfolio roadmap, leadership actions*. Each zone shows current targets, gaps, progress, and problems, color-coded red/green. Each IT objective ties measurably to enterprise strategy.
  - When to use: to make the status of strategic objectives instantly visible to leaders, teams, and visitors.
  - How: hold regular-cadence meetings there with direct reports. Anyone can visit between meetings to update or review.
  - Why it works: problems become visible immediately, and strategy and execution share one surface. Failure mode: copying the walls without the behaviors. "Transformational, generative leadership extends well beyond what is on the Obeya walls."
- **Stand-up + Catchball rhythm** (leader standard work): squads hold ~15-minute daily stand-ups at a visual board (demand, WIP, done, obstacles). Problems are *noted, not solved* in the stand-up, then routed: peer issue → discussed later that day; needs the IT-area lead → escalated (that lead may take it to their own stand-up with other IT-area leads or tribe leads); technical issue → the relevant chapter or center of expertise. Resolutions flow back through the same channel, and the problem stays visualized until resolved. This vertical and horizontal flow is "catchball."
  - Why it works: learning moves both ways. Squads learn strategic priorities, and leaders learn about the customer experience from frontline interactions. Meeting time dropped significantly compared with before.
- **Strategy deployment (Hoshin Kanri) + PDCA**: the catchball exchange is a form of strategy deployment. It creates a continuous, rapid feedback cycle of learning, testing, validating, and adjusting (Plan-Do-Check-Act) at all levels.
- **Leaders-as-coaches: three jobs for everyone**: (1) do the work, (2) improve the work, (3) develop the people. Leaders visit squads and ask "Help me better understand the problems you're encountering," "Help me see what you're learning," and "What can I do to better support you and the team?" instead of "Why isn't this getting done?"
- **ING problem-solving approach** (a blend of A3, Kata, Lean startup): (1) gather the people with experience of the problem; (2) rigorously examine the current condition to find root cause, not symptoms; (3) form a hypothesis, including how and what to measure; (4) run the experiment; (5) if it succeeds, make it standard work, share the learning, and keep monitoring. Senior-level problems are broken down and cascaded to chapter or squad level for frontline experiments, and the learning feeds back up.
- **Standard work (process vs. practice)**: *process* is the way something is done (e.g., Scrum rituals). *Practice* is doing something that requires knowledge and judgment (understanding customer needs, writing code). A team experiments, agrees on the one best way, and spreads it to similar teams. The standard is re-evaluated when conditions change. It is never imported from a book or another company.
- **Mindset guidance for your own path**: develop and maintain the right mindset (learning, not practices or tools); **make it your own** (don't copy other enterprises or expert models; don't contract the transformation out to a large consulting firm; do develop your own coaches, since coaching depth is the key lever for sustaining and scaling); you, too, need to change your way of work (lead by example, don't delegate); practice discipline; practice patience; practice practice ("rhythm and routine").

## Key Concepts
- **Tribe**: a line-of-business unit delivering a portfolio of related products and services, aligned to a value stream (e.g., Mortgage Services Tribe).
- **Squad**: a self-steering, cross-functional team with a distinct customer mission. It is guided by a product owner, led (in IT) by an IT-area lead, and sized by Bezos' **Two Pizza Rule**.
- **BizDevOps**: ING's term for squads that combine engineers and marketers with a shared understanding of customer value.
- **Chapter**: members of the same discipline, matrixed across squads, who spread specialized knowledge (e.g., Data Analytics Chapter).
- **Center of expertise**: people with a particular capability (e.g., communications, enterprise architects).
- **Product area lead**: an emergent bridging role aligning closely related squads. It came from experience, not from the plan.
- **Lean Leadership Expertise Squad**: a small group of internal improvement coaches who coach leaders, chapter leads, product owners, and IT-area leads. Those people in turn coach their members, which creates leverage at scale.
- **Team security / psychological safety**: the ability to discuss problems openly without fear of harm or reprisal.
- **Implementation thinking**: mimicking another company's practices. It runs counter to a generative culture.
- **"Going deep before going wide"**: grow capability in a few places before scaling (ING operates in 40+ countries).

## Mental Models
- Think of culture as the output of routines. To change culture, change the way of work: visual management, stand-ups, coaching.
- Use "slow down to speed up" when pressure for speed threatens quality. The leader grants explicit permission and safety to put quality first.
- Think of the organization as a learning network: catchball carries learning vertically (strategy ↔ frontline) and horizontally (squads ↔ chapters ↔ centers of expertise).
- Ask "How do we learn how to learn?" instead of "How do we change our culture?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Copying another company's practices or "implementing" an expert framework**: there is no checklist or playbook, and you can't "implement" culture change.
- **Outsourcing transformation to a large consultancy**: teams feel Lean or Agile is being done *to* them. Processes may improve temporarily, but teams lack the capability to sustain and adapt.
- **Command-and-control questioning** ("Why isn't this getting done?"): kills learning and safety.
- **Solving problems in the stand-up**: bloats it. Note, route, and visualize instead.
- **Scaling wide and fast** (e.g., an Obeya for every senior leader at once): spreads the artifacts without real learning.
- **Asking others to change first**: "As a leader, you have to look at your own behaviors before you ask others to change." (Jannes Smit)
- **Neglecting "develop the people" amid automation**: people won't automate away their own jobs unless they trust leaders to value their ability to learn and innovate.

## Worked Example
**ING Netherlands, Jannes Smit's tribes.** ING is a global bank with 34.4M+ customers, 52,000 employees, and 9,000+ engineers. Seven years earlier, Jannes Smit (IT Manager, Internet Banking and Omnichannel) saw that his teams were not learning or improving: one team wrestled with a problem another team had already solved. His insight: "When we were not able to learn as management, we were not able to help the teams to learn." Steps:
1. He brought in coaches, gathered his management team ("Let's try this together. Even if it doesn't work, we will learn something"), and held quarterly **boot camps**.
2. Leader standard work: visual management, regular stand-ups, and consistent coaching replaced long meetings and fire-fighting. First, each manager **recorded how they spent their time for several weeks**, shared the results, and co-designed new routines.
3. Result three months later: "I'll never go back to the old way of working again!" (Mark Nijssen). The team also freed **10% of their time** for work of their choice.
4. Under pressure to be "twice as effective" on a tough deadline, Jannes told the squads: "If the quality isn't there, don't release. I'll cover your back." The squads felt they owned quality.
5. A fixed percentage of each squad's and chapter's time is allocated to improvement and treated as regular work.
6. Outcome: IT moved from offsite to one floor below the C-suite and now leads ING's digital transformation. "We give them speed with quality... once we achieve [green], we tend to stay green, when a lot of the others go back to red."

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with yourself: the leader and the management team learn to be a learning team before coaching others.
2. Install leader standard work: visual management (Obeya), short stand-ups, catchball escalation, and coaching questions.
3. Connect every team objective measurably to enterprise strategy, and let learning flow both up and down (Hoshin Kanri/PDCA).
4. Protect quality explicitly. Leaders give teams cover to not release poor quality.
5. Reserve a fixed share of capacity for improvement, and run improvements as measured experiments that become standard work.
6. Build internal coaching depth and go deep before going wide. Don't outsource or copy.
7. Practice discipline, patience, and practice. Habits become culture.
